Northwestern Formula Racing 2027:
Corner Brokers

The Corner Brokers are four small boards, one at each wheel, that read the sensors at that corner and publish the readings on the car's CAN bus. I'm the layout lead and primary designer for this year's version (NFR27), taking it over from a senior who designed NFR26 and has since graduated. It's the first PCB I've designed from a blank schematic, and I'm responsible for it from schematic through firmware, bring-up, and getting it on the car.

Altium Designer Schematic Design PCB Layout STM32 Strain Gauges CAN Bus Python

1. Overview

One Board, Four Corners

Four boards form a distributed sensor network, one per wheel. Each reads pushrod and tie-rod strain gauges, a suspension potentiometer, and a brake rotor temperature sensor, conditions each signal, and publishes the readings to the vehicle CAN network.

The point is to measure the suspension under real driving loads. That gives the team data to validate future designs against, and it provides the per-corner load feedback that torque vectoring needs.

Inheriting a board is a strange starting point. NFR26 worked, mostly, so every change I make has to earn its place against a known-good design. A lot of my decisions this year came down to that: take the lower-risk option unless the old board had a real problem.

2. Starting From Data

Reading Last Year's Logs

Before picking parts, I went through NFR26's drive-day logs in Python to see what the sensors had actually recorded on track. Those numbers, not the datasheets, are what I used to make the sensor and gain decisions below.

3. Hardware

What Changed From NFR26

The schematic is in Altium, split across hierarchical sheets for Power, CAN, ADC, MCU, and Connectors. Every board has to fit a 38.5 × 21 mm envelope so it can mount at the corner, and that constraint drove most of the part choices. I designed the analog front end for each sensor type, choosing the amplifier topology and filter response for each signal path. Here are the main changes from last year and why I made them:

BlockDecision
MCUSTM32L432KCU6 (UFQFPN32) in place of last year's STM32F405. The board doesn't need the F405's pin count, and the smaller package frees up a lot of space.
ADCADS1232. The ADS1235 and ADS1220 are better parts on paper, but the ADS1232 matches a design we know works and costs less across four boards.
PowerTPSM33615 buck module feeding a TLV1117LV33 LDO. Last year's linear regulators ran hot; the buck does most of the step-down so the LDO only has to cover the last bit.
CANTCAN3403DRQ1 transceiver, with the TVS protection repositioned.
Reverse polarityFDN5618P PMOS, an in-stock substitute for a part with a 40-week lead time. I added a TVS gate clamp after design review.

4. Tradeoffs

DC vs. AC Excitation

NFR26's strain readings drifted, and AC excitation is the textbook fix: flipping the bridge polarity cancels out thermocouple offsets and some slow drift. It also costs board area, parts, and complexity, and this board is supposed to be small and cheap to build four times.

I stayed with DC excitation. To reduce the zero-drift error we measured on last year's boards, I'm reworking the strain channel and improving gauge bonding instead. I gave up AC's drift advantage to get a smaller footprint and a lower cost.

5. Debugging

The Clipping Bug

While working through the schematic I found that the bridges were excited at 5V but the ADC's reference was 3.3V. The front end could put out more signal than the reference allowed, so readings were clipping at full scale. Tracking that down meant following the excitation and reference rails through the whole signal path instead of checking parts one at a time.
