Northwestern Formula Racing 2027:
(E)Education Board

A CAN node PCB built for teaching, not racing. New members solder it, bring up its power tree, get it talking on CAN, debug its firmware, and read real sensors off it. It's also meant to be the schematic template for the subteam's future sensor boards. I'm designing it with Benson Zhang and helping build the onboarding curriculum around it. We treat teachability as a design requirement on the same level as correctness.

Altium Designer Schematic Design PCB Layout STM32 ΔΣ ADC CAN Bus Design Review

1. Overview

A Board That Explains Itself

Most of what new members need to learn on DAQ comes from real boards: through-hole and SMD soldering, bringing up a power tree one rail at a time, CAN communication, firmware debug, and getting clean sensor data. The board puts all of that into a single build, and new members also take it from Altium schematic to layout themselves.

Designing for teaching changed a lot of choices. A clever circuit that takes an hour to explain loses to a plain one that makes the idea obvious. Our constraints: no split ground planes, roughly the same board size as last year's, and every block should be readable by someone seeing it for the first time.

2. Architecture

Current Design

The core is an STM32, a CAN transceiver, input protection, and an external ADC. That's the same skeleton as most of our sensor boards, which is why it doubles as the reference schematic for them.

3. Design History

From Load Cell to FSR

The first version had a TAL220 load cell in a Wheatstone bridge read by an ADS1231. I redrew it as a quarter-bridge and worked through porting the ADS1231's interface, which turned out not to be standard SPI. It's a two-wire clocked shift-out with no MOSI or chip select and a 1 MHz clock ceiling.

Eventually we swapped the whole front end for an FSR divider into an MCP3561R. A bridge amplifier with a fixed gain of 128 is a lot to explain on someone's first day. A voltage divider into a general-purpose ADC teaches the same acquisition chain (sensor, filter, ADC, MCU) with far less to explain first.

4. Power

One PMOS, Three Lessons

The board runs from the car's GLV rail or from USB-C. When 12V is present, a divider pulls the PMOS gate high so USB VBUS can't backfeed into the rest of the board. We use that same PMOS topology for reverse-polarity protection and for OR-ing the two sources. That was on purpose: once someone understands one PMOS circuit, they can read all three.

5. Layout Plan

Zones by Return Current

4-layer, 1.6mm, 85 × 60mm, with every part on the top side. The bottom is only ground pour, stitching, and silkscreen, so there's one side to solder and one side to label.

  • Five placement zones grouped by return-current domain: analog (ADC/FSR), digital core (MCU/IMU), power, CAN + SWD, and USB + user I/O
  • Stackup: top signals / solid ground on L2 with no splits / rail pours and slow signals on L3 / routing plus ground on L4
  • No moat around analog ground. AGND merges into GND as one net, and digital routing is kept out of the analog zone instead
  • No controlled impedance. USB and CAN edge rates are too slow for traces this short to matter

6. Review

What Design Review Caught

We've been through a schematic review, a punch list, and two passes of a layout plan. The most recent review turned up several blocker-level issues that have to be fixed before placement:

  • Two MCUs on one sheet. The main symbol is the U535, but a leftover power-pin part still carries the old F405 footprint. It has to be merged away before any layout can start.
  • A net-name break. The switcher output and LDO input have different net names, so as drawn the 3.3V rail never gets power.
  • SWD pinout. SWDIO and SWCLK are swapped, and pin 3 is tied to 3.3V where the ARM Cortex Debug standard puts GND. A standard cable would short the probe's ground to the rail.
  • Pin map check. Confirming the UFQFPN-48 pinout against the datasheet before committing to the rotated MCU in the floorplan, and remapping two signals to the package side that faces their zone.
  • Filter cleanup. Removing a redundant NRST RC, and replacing an ADC-supply ferrite whose LC with the bulk caps peaks about 12dB near 65kHz. A plain 10Ω resistor gives a real filter with no peaking.

7. Cost

BOM

The raw BOM export wasn't usable for pricing: some rows showed per-1000 prices that were never divided down, and the three main ICs had no price at all. After cleaning it up, a single board comes to about $40.51, and we expect around $28–32 per board at the 10–25 unit quantities a team order would use.
